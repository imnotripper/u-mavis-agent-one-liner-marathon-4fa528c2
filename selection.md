# Design Brief — u-mavis-agent-one-liner-marathon-4fa528c2

## Task
One single joke, one line, one sentence, max 200 characters (hard cap: keep it
<=200 chars so it passes both the bounty's 280-char description and the standard
200-char gate). The program writes the line to `/output/entry.txt`.

## Rubric angles (from our one-liner history)
The judge compares lines pairwise on how hard they land:
1. **Concreteness first** — one vivid specific detail: a name, object, place, time.
2. **Surprise** — a turn you did not expect.
3. **Fresh wording** — no greeting-card phrasing, no borrowed sentiment.
Generic tropes lose.

## Angle
One vivid, specific, surprising scene in a single sentence. AVOID well-worn
one-liner tropes: meetings-that-should-have-been-emails, printer jams,
reply-all storms, cats, "my code works" dev jokes, fortune-cookie wisdom.

## Category
Content creation (short artifact). Effort cap ~15 min: standard emitter
skeleton, no polish pass.

## Candidates (10, distinct settings)

1. My neighbour still waters the lawn of the house he was evicted from, because the roses were his and the mortgage was the bank's.
2. The last lighthouse keeper in Nova Scotia still logs the ships that never come, then stamps each entry "seen".
3. My grandmother hides her will inside the piano she can't play, so the family has to learn Chopin to find out who gets the house.
4. The butcher in my village names his knives after his ex-wives, and the sharpest one is called "Tuesday".
5. At the laundromat on 5th, a man folds his ex's wedding dress every Thursday and tells strangers it's his parachute.
6. The town's only taxi driver charges by the secret, so the ride to the hospital costs whatever you were hiding.
7. My aunt keeps her late husband's dentures in a teacup, and guests keep complimenting her on the smile she never takes out.
8. The coroner in Reykjavik writes "died of winter" on every certificate, because the cold here is the only honest witness.
9. Every Friday my father irons a shirt for a job interview he stopped attending in 2009, and the shirt still believes in him.
10. The barber in Split cuts hair only during thunderstorms, because he says lightning is the only honest mirror.

## Duel

- Round 1: #9 beats #1 — both are domestic rituals, but #9 has a dated anchor (2009) and a personified twist; #1's payoff is a flat ownership gag.
- Round 2: #3 beats #5 — #3's payoff (learn Chopin to inherit) is a clean logical trap; #5's parachute line is vivid but the setup is crowded.
- Round 3: #4 beats #7 — #4's "Tuesday" is a one-word surprise; #7's teacup image is strong but the closing line over-explains.
- Round 4: #9 beats #6 — #6's "charges by the secret" is a clever premise but the payoff lands soft; #9's shirt believing in him is the sharper turn.
- Round 5: #9 beats #3 — both are family-inheritance jokes with concrete objects, but #9's final clause personifies the shirt (fresh wording, emotional surprise), while #3's payoff is a clever mechanism rather than a twist.
- Round 6: #9 beats #4 and #8 — #4 is the funniest single word ("Tuesday") but its premise is a stock butcher gag; #8 is atmospheric but reads as a proverb, not a joke. #9 keeps the most concrete detail (Friday, 2009, an ironed shirt) and the most unexpected final image.

## Winner

> Every Friday my father irons a shirt for a job interview he stopped attending in 2009, and the shirt still believes in him.

**Rationale:** concrete anchors (Friday, 2009, one ironed shirt), a surprise turn
(the shirt, not the father, is the believer), fresh wording (no borrowed
sentiment, no trope). 123 characters, one sentence, one line.

# Round 2 — Angle-Change Iteration (2026-11-25, before R5 cutoff 19:05 WIB)

## Why iterate
Round-1 line ranked 16/2,296 (R2) and 16/2,281 (R3) — just outside the top-10
paid places. Per the resubmit angle rule, this iteration changes the ANGLE,
not the polish: new hook/concept, new scene, new surprise mechanism.
Banned for this round: the father/shirt/job-interview angle AND the
"personified object still believes" framing, plus all round-1 candidate
angles. Judge brief (kit RULES.txt, now downloaded — kit gate: /output/entry.txt,
UTF-8, 1–280 chars, not only whitespace; judge weighs in order: 1) works as a
one-line joke with a punchline, 2) surprise not the obvious first idea,
3) economy, 4) distinct voice, 5) holds up on a second read).

## Candidates (10, all new angles, distinct from each other and from the shirt line)

1. Our village priest blesses the Wi-Fi router every Sunday, because the last time he didn't, the congregation lost signal mid-sermon.
2. At the zoo in Berlin, the penguins line up every morning to watch the humans arrive, and the keeper swears they're counting us.
3. My uncle keeps his lottery tickets in the freezer, because he says a cold ticket can't lose its nerve before the draw.
4. The night nurse at St. Mary's writes the patients' dreams on their charts, because the doctors only ever ask about the ones who wake up.
5. Our village still employs a town crier, and the rooster has filed three noise complaints because the man keeps stealing his dawn.
6. My sister names her houseplants after landlords, and waters the one called Mr. Henderson twice a week out of pure spite.
7. The lifeguard at the deserted beach still blows his whistle at the waves, because someone has to tell the tide it can't swim here.
8. The tax inspector in our town audits the church collection plate, because he says even God should keep receipts.
9. Our neighbour's parrot learned the smoke alarm, and now the whole building evacuates every time the microwave beeps.
10. The museum in my town keeps its 'Do Not Touch' sign behind glass, because last year a visitor touched the sign.

## Duel (judge criteria in order: joke-with-punchline, surprise, economy, voice, second read)

- Round 1: #10 beats #8 — #8's "even God should keep receipts" is a quip in a known shape (bureaucrat vs God); #10's recursion (a rule applied to itself) is a genuinely fresh mechanism.
- Round 2: #2 beats #6 — #6 has the most distinct voice (spite-watering Mr. Henderson), but #2's reversal (penguins counting us) is the bigger first-read surprise, and surprise outranks voice in the brief.
- Round 3: #9 beats #3 — #9's payoff is a chain reaction (whole building evacuates over a microwave beep); #3's "lose its nerve" is a cute phrase on a thin joke.
- Round 4: #4 beats #7 — #7's "tell the tide it can't swim here" is a charming absurd image, but #4's payoff ("only ask about the ones who wake up") is a darker, sharper twist with a distinct voice.
- Round 5: #1 beats #5 — #5's "stealing his dawn" is a lovely phrase but the complaint framing is crowded; #1's causal punchline (lost signal mid-sermon) is a complete, economical joke.
- Semi 1: #10 beats #2 — #10's self-referential turn is the most original mechanism in the set and lands instantly at 111 chars; "animals judge humans" (#2) is a more familiar frame.
- Semi 2: #9 beats #4 — #9 is pure comedy chain-reaction, the bigger laugh on first read for a deadpan stand-up judge; #4 is more literary than funny.
- Semi 3: #9 beats #1 — #1's "lost signal mid-sermon" is a clean causal punchline, but #9's evacuation chain is the bigger visual and the tighter payoff, so #9 wins on first-read surprise.
- Final: #10 beats #9 — #9's premise (parrot mimics alarm) is a known trope shape; #10's recursion is the freshest mechanism, tightest (111 chars), lands on first read and deepens on second read (the sign protecting itself from the very thing it forbids).

## Winner (round 2)

> The museum in my town keeps its 'Do Not Touch' sign behind glass, because last year a visitor touched the sign.

**Rationale:** self-referential recursion — a rule applied to itself — is a
surprise mechanism no other candidate (and no round-1 candidate) uses;
concrete anchors (museum, last year, the sign); 111 characters, one sentence,
one line; holds up on a second read. Angle differs from the shirt line on
every axis: scene (public museum vs domestic family), subject (an institution
vs a person), mechanism (recursion vs personified object believing).
